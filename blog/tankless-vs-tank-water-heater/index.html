    Tankless vs. Tank Water Heaters: What Works Best in Utah

    By Travis Baines · September 18, 2026 · 6 min read

    Two Ways to Heat Water

    A tank heater keeps 40 to 80 gallons hot around the clock. A tankless unit heats water only while a tap is open. Both can be gas or electric, both can serve a whole house, and the choice usually comes down to how your family uses hot water and what your gas line and water will support. There is no single winner.

    The Tank Heater

    Tanks are simple and cheap to replace. A standard 50-gallon gas tank swap is usually a same-day job. The tradeoff is standby loss, since the burner or elements cycle all day to keep stored water hot whether anyone is home or not, and the tank holds a fixed amount, so the fourth shower in a row runs cold. Most tanks last somewhere between 8 and 12 years.

    Tanks also forgive a lot. They tolerate a small gas line and moderate scale without complaint. If the power goes out, a gas tank with a standing pilot keeps making hot water, and the stored 50 gallons can even serve as an emergency supply drawn from the drain valve. That counts for something during a storm.

    Fast growth means much of the housing in Utah is relatively new, with PEX supply lines and plastic drains in most recent construction.

    The Tankless Heater

    Tankless heaters are rated by flow, in gallons per minute at a given temperature rise. The bigger gas models top out around 8 to 9 gallons a minute on mild incoming water. Colder incoming water means a bigger temperature rise, so the same unit delivers less flow in winter, and two showers plus a dishwasher can push a small unit past what it can keep up with. Get the size right first.

    The upside is real. You get continuous hot water, provided the unit was sized for the busiest hour in your house. Without standby loss, a gas tankless unit typically burns noticeably less fuel than a tank, it hangs on a wall instead of eating closet floor space, and a well-kept unit can run 20 years. The heat exchanger is the part to protect.

    Maintenance looks different on each. A tank wants a flush once a year through the drain valve to clear sediment. The anode rod, a sacrificial magnesium or aluminum rod that keeps the steel from rusting, should be checked every three to five years, and replacing a spent anode is one of the cheapest ways to get extra years out of a tank. On a tankless unit, the yearly job is descaling plus cleaning the inlet filter.

    What Changes the Math in a Utah Home

    Utah water is among the hardest in the country in many areas, and scale builds up quickly in water heaters, faucets and appliances.

    Installation is where tankless costs climb. Expect a larger gas line, often 3/4-inch, plus new venting rated for the unit. Electric tankless models for a whole house can need 120 amps or more on dedicated breakers, which many older panels simply do not have room for, so a panel upgrade sometimes rides along with the job. We look at the panel and the gas meter first.

    Hard water is the other big variable. Mineral buildup on the heat exchanger cuts flow and throws fault codes. On hard water, plan to descale the unit every year with a small pump and a descaling solution circulated through the isolation valves, or put a softener ahead of it. A tank collects the same minerals as sediment on the bottom.

    An arid climate with hot summers, cold winters and big daily temperature swings puts steady stress on plumbing across Utah.

    Family size and habits decide the rest. A couple in a small house rarely runs two showers at once. A family of five with teenagers and a soaking tub is a different load entirely, and that house needs either a large tankless unit sized for three fixtures at once or a bigger tank with a fast recovery rate. The first question is how many showers run together.

    Code details matter on either type. A tank needs a temperature and pressure relief valve piped to a safe discharge point. Gas units of both kinds need proper combustion air and venting, and a tankless unit needs isolation valves so it can be flushed without cutting into the plumbing every year. Skipping those shows up on inspection.

    Ask about recovery rate when comparing tanks. A gas tank reheats a full load much faster than an electric one. First-hour rating, printed on the yellow energy label, tells you how many gallons a tank delivers in the first hour of heavy use, and it is a better number to compare than tank size alone for a busy house. Hybrid heat pump tanks are another option.

    When to Call a Professional

    A replacement is the cheapest time to switch. A tank past 10 years or weeping at the base is worth pricing both ways now.
