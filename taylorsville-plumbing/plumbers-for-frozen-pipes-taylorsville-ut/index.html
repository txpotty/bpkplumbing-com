    Costs range from about $200 for simple thawing to $1,500 or more for a burst pipe inside a finished wall. Access drives the price. A frozen line in an open crawlspace is quick work, while a split pipe in a second-floor ceiling means opening drywall, repairing the pipe and leaving the opening ready for a drywall patch.

    Usually not for a simple repair. Larger repiping may need one. If we replace a long section of supply line, reroute pipes into a different wall, or replace a backflow assembly, your jurisdiction may require a permit and an inspection, and we schedule it for you.

    You can open the faucet and gently warm an exposed pipe with a hair dryer. Never use a torch. If the frozen section is inside a wall, a ceiling or under the floor, call a plumber instead, because you cannot see whether it has already split, and thawing it can release water into the structure.

    A frost-free hose bib has a long stem that shuts off water inside the warm part of the wall. The outside part drains when you close it. It only works if the hose is disconnected, because a connected hose traps water in the stem where it can freeze and split the valve inside the wall, which you may not notice until spring.

    Most thawing jobs take one to two hours. Fixing a burst usually takes a few hours. Repairs inside finished ceilings or walls take longer because we open the drywall, repair the pipe, test it and leave the opening clean and ready for a patch, and multiple splits on one run add time as well.

    A short, exposed section usually thaws in 30 to 60 minutes. Pipes inside walls take more time. Lines in walls or plastic pipe can take a few hours with gentler heat.

    Keep cold air away from the pipe. Insulation and air sealing do most of the work. Close crawlspace vents for the season, insulate attic and garage runs, swap old hose bibs for frost-free models, and add self-regulating heat cable on any run that still gets too cold despite insulation.
