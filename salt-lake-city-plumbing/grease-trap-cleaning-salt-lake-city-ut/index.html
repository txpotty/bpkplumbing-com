    Slow sinks come first. Gurgling floor drains and a sour smell by the dish station usually follow. Gray water rising through a floor sink means the trap is far past 25 percent and grease is already moving downstream.

    Staff can clean a small under-sink trap between pump-outs. The waste still needs legal disposal. A common setup is daily skimming by staff and a full pump and scrape from us every few weeks, so the records stay complete.

    Watch the floor sinks during dish rush. Standing water in a floor sink is the first hint. Other signs include a rancid smell near the dish station, grease showing up in the cleanout nearest the street, and drains that gurgle after the dish machine empties.

    Everything in the trap comes out, from the floating grease cap down to the sludge of food solids on the floor. Haulers call it brown grease. Yellow grease, the used fryer oil in your collection bin, is a separate product that gets recycled and should never go into the trap.

    Skimming off the top layer yourself is usually allowed. Disposing of it is where people get in trouble. Pretreatment programs often require a licensed hauler's record for each pump-out, so a kitchen that handles its own cleaning may have no paperwork to show when the inspector asks.

    No permit is needed just to have a trap pumped. The records are your responsibility. Installing a new trap or interceptor, or changing the size of one, usually needs a plumbing permit and sometimes a pretreatment program review before the work starts.
